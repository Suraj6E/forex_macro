Timestamp validation

Every number this project produces is anchored to a release timestamp a source handed us, and a timestamp wrong by an hour still yields a decay curve with a significance level attached — wrong, and entirely plausible. The only witness is price itself: if a release matters, the pair that carries its currency moves when it lands. This screen is where price gets to disagree with the calendar.

Press the button above. Reading is all it does — the grades are stored on the releases, and nothing else is touched.

Readable counts the releases whose surrounding price action is this indicator's to interpret. A bar of the scan cannot win it if a release at least as important sits in that bar — run without that rule, the check named thirty-nine indicators as systematically offset and every one of them turned out to be pointing at a bigger neighbour: European morning prints at +3h, which is the 13:30 UTC US cluster, and 10:00 New York prints at −2h, which is the 08:30 one.

Median move is the release hour's range as a multiple of what that pair normally does in the same weekday-and-hour slot; anything at or above {{ spike_ratio }}× counts as a spike. The comparison skips look-back windows holding another release of the same indicator — without that, a weekly series is compared against itself and every ratio collapses towards 1.

Not the same question as a price gap. A month of bars can be complete and still leave a release ungradable, because the comparison needs twelve matched weekday-and-hour slots behind it — so the first weeks of the price history have nothing to be normal against.

What this check can and cannot say

The price backbone is hourly, so the affirmative grade here is confirmed at hour resolution: the spike landed in the release's own hour. §5.3's stronger grade — within ±2 minutes — needs M1 bars around each event and stays reserved for that check, so the two are never conflated in the database.

That weaker grade still catches the failure that actually threatens this dataset. A daylight-saving error, a source stamping local time, or an off-by-one in our own conversion all displace a release by a whole hour or more, and an hourly scan resolves every one of them.

It also cannot separate this release from a bigger one three hours either side of it, so it does not try: when the spike belongs to a heavier neighbour the grade is attribution refused, and that release votes on nothing. Conservative on purpose — a real offset whose hour happens to hold a big release is missed rather than reported, because a check that exists to find our own bugs is worthless if it cries wolf.

A single release's grade is noisy and is not meant to be read alone: one hour of FX can be quiet for reasons that have nothing to do with the release. The signal is the pooled shape — a wrong clock puts most of an indicator's spikes on the same wrong offset.
